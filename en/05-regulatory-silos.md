# 05. Regulatory Silos, Suppressed Price Signals and the Fallacy of Composition

> **⚡ Transferable rule:** **Three patches in a row means rebuild.** Consecutive administrative fixes are the symptom of a decision-maker who doesn't dare say "no" to users — and each patch multiplies the system's internal friction.

## 📖 What the book actually says

- **Regulatory silos + fallacy of composition:** each department guards its own local indicator (microprudential) while nobody holds the system-wide, look-through balance sheet (macroprudential). Cross-border institutions leverage up precisely in the vacuum between jurisdictions — **the sum of local compliance is aggregate destruction.**
- **The instinctive crisis response makes it worse:** each silo's first move is to slam its own fire door to prove its own cleanliness — cutting the escape routes for everyone.
- **The burden-sharing dilemma:** order the compliant department to shrink 30% "for the building's safety" and it will rightly ask why its perfect audit score should pay for someone else's powder keg. System-wide caps without a cost-sharing rule are politically dead on arrival.
- **Patches breed shadow markets:** administrative quotas never eliminate arbitrage; they push it to the boundary and grow a larger shadow market. Every patch raises the system's friction and entropy exponentially.
- **Price signal suppression:** refusing to let prices float is usually about protecting a mental illusion — "always available, always cheap." The cost doesn't vanish; it re-emerges as worker resentment, wasted system capacity, and arbitrage holes.
- **Regulatory forbearance:** officials keep insolvent institutions open "to protect depositors," but the real calculus is brutal — close it and the angry crowd blocks your door today; secretly transfuse it and the hole is diluted into years of inflation and stagnation that nobody can attribute to you. **Decision-makers will accept a hundredfold systemic loss rather than one named, dated accusation.**

> *"The cost of daring to refuse is local, controllable, short-term pain. The cost of not daring to refuse is endless net-welfare leakage for everyone inside a slow-failing system."*

## 💡 How it rewired my decisions

- **Before:** in the delivery-platform wargame I personally built the five-patch ladder — coefficient subsidies → forced quotas → privilege swaps → weekly caps → staggered resets — each one fixing the loophole the last one created, until the platform resembled a Soviet planning ministry.
- **After:** replaying the chain, I admitted the true reason I never took the market path: *fear that users would leave when surge pricing made delivery expensive.* Suppression wasn't protecting the system — it was protecting my illusion and my reputation.
- **The hard rule that survived:** stop patching at three; return to the mechanism. And **let cold rules play the bad guy** — pre-written circuit breakers (e.g., courier shortage > 60% → intake closes) so no individual has to bear the moral weight of saying no in real time.
- **The asymmetry I learned:** humans punish sins of action far more than sins of omission. Actively closing orders gets you 100% of the blame; letting the system congest gets the weather the blame. Choosing suppression is choosing to exploit that bias — at the system's expense.
