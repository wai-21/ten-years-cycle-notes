# 06. Implicit Guarantees and Cross-Guarantees: The Too-Big-to-Fail Hostage Structure

> **⚡ Transferable rule:** **Give equity, never credit; give ammunition, never a fuse.** Limited liability is a wall in law and a sheet of paper in the credit market — one cross-default clause cuts through it.

## 📖 What the book actually says

- **Too-big-to-fail is a reverse hostage right.** Once a module's failure is priced as "unbearable for the whole system," it acquires leverage over the top decision-maker. Under "gains mine, losses everyone's," it will deliberately push toward极限 risk — until the host is fully bound.
- **Cross-guarantees detonate the ring-fence.** The whole point of separate legal entities is risk isolation. Cross-guarantees weave the group into one unlimited joint-liability net: book assets multiply, but **everyone in effect shares the same thin capital**. When the most marginal, most speculative unit blows, the healthiest cash cow is dragged down with it.
- **Tunneling kills the healthy organ.** In a squeeze, the controlling family's instinct is to transfuse the cash cow's blood into zombie projects — saving neither, while draining the working capital of the only businesses that worked, upgrading a local loss into a chain-wide break.
- **Equity is a sponge; debt is a noose.** Equity lets a firm sit out the winter without dividends or principal; but investors will only accept unlimited downside in exchange for transparent audits, board seats, veto rights on related-party deals, and the power to sack management.
- **A pure equity split is just paper.** Banks reach the parent anyway through cross-default clauses and "letters of liquidity support" — and even with clean documents, reputational contagion fires indiscriminately.
- **The only real cure — equity as a sunk option:**
  - **Be an angel investor, never a guarantor:** support is a one-time pure equity grant, booked as 100% loss on day one. No top-up undertakings, no support letters, no joint bank guarantees.
  - **Non-recourse financing, physically separated:** the subsidiary finances itself only from its own assets or project cash flows; external creditors sign legally hard non-recourse terms.

> *"Every financial crisis is, at bottom, a governance crisis."* — Andrew Sheng's thesis for the whole book.

## 💡 How it rewired my decisions

- **Before:** on "will the subsidiary's blowup kill the parent," my answer was the business-school standard — separate legal entity, strict limited liability.
- **After:** in the credit market that's a thin sheet. Demand full independence and the subsidiary's rating collapses (12%+ pricing, full collateral); to get cheap money management signs support letters and cross-default terms, and one clause ends the firewall. My fallback — "then I'll only back hard assets like property" — was worse: property is the most levered, most procyclical, least liquid amplifier in the economy, and defending an asset you cannot sell while paying its carrying costs is casting your own steel coffin.
- **The line I hold now:** when backing new ventures — equity, no credit; ammunition, no fuse. And in the "Old Lei" wargame (a partner with 65% of revenue and forged company seals), the 48-hour playbook: use the criminal evidence to force internal surrender first, then confront the shadow creditors with the fact that the guarantee was forged — turning their expectation from "recover 30M" to "recover nothing" before offering the discount.
